About Anene

A songbook for Kiribati music. Anene is Gilbertese for a song, and for singing one. Everything below is the reasoning behind a decision this site made on your behalf — including the things it deliberately will not do.

What it is

Static pages. Each song is a file in the repository and a real HTML document on the server — no app, no account, no database. Open a page and the words are there; it works with JavaScript switched off, and it keeps working offline once you have visited it.

There is no score, no streak and no progress bar. A songbook is finished when it has the songs you want in it.

Why the Find page has no lyrics on it

Find lists 149 songs, indexed from kirilyrics.net on 2026-08-01. It carries titles, credits, and a link to the page each came from — nothing else, on purpose.

Those words were composed by people, and written down and shared by contributors who took the time to do it. Copying that collection onto this site would be taking their work and serving it as though it were ours. So the index does the one thing it honestly can: it tells you the song exists, what it is called, and where it is documented. Anything in the songbook itself was put there by whoever runs this copy of the site, out of what they had the right to publish.

Why search is loose about spelling

Gilbertese has no single enforced orthography. Spelling varies by island, by era, and by whether the writer learned it from a mission primer or from their grandmother. The public index this site draws its Find page from credits one contributor three different ways on neighbouring pages — Tiimi, Tiimii, Tiimiii — and it is not wrong to; that is just how it is written.

So search folds before it compares:

  • Diacritics are stripped — macrons are written inconsistently, when at all.
  • bw → b and mw → m, so maneaba finds mwaneaba and Banieta finds Bwanieta.
  • Any repeated letter collapses to one, so Tiimiii, Tiimi and Timi are the same search.

The last rule genuinely over-matches: vowel length carries meaning in Gilbertese, and folding it throws that away. That is a deliberate trade. The cost of one unexpected extra result is a glance; the cost of not finding a song you know is on the site is that you stop trusting the search, and then you stop using the book.

What is not folded: ti stays ti, even though it is pronounced /s/. Folding it would collide unrelated words for the sake of a spelling almost nobody writes, and a match you cannot explain is worse than a miss you can.

Folding happens only when matching. What was typed into the song file is what is published — this site is in no position to arbitrate anyone's spelling, and it does not try.

How translations line up

A blank line starts a new verse. When a song and its translation have the same number of verses, the two are shown together, verse by verse — and within a verse, line by line, but only when those line counts match too.

When they don't match, the page says so and shows the two texts one after the other instead. It will not guess. Pairing verse three's meaning with verse two's words would teach the song wrong, and you would have no way of noticing — a gap you can see beats a mistake you can't.

What it can't tell you

  • Whether the words are right. There is no authority here — no dictionary, no correction, no canonical version. Two islands will sing a line differently and both are the song.
  • Whether a translation is good. Nothing is machine-translated and nothing is checked.
  • Who wrote a song. The Find index repeats credits as the source states them, and the source contradicts itself in a few places. Where it does, both readings are left standing rather than one being quietly chosen.

Small things

  • Lyrics are set in a serif at five sizes, because they get read across a room. The size you pick is remembered.
  • A verse is labelled by putting the label in square brackets on its own first line — [Kaotinako], [Verse 2].
